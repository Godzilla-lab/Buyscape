# Teach a small model honesty, not prices

The research from 2023 to 2026 points to one clear answer. A small open vision-language model (a VLM: a model that reads images and text together) can be fine-tuned well for Buyscape's narrow task. But it works only if the training teaches **behaviour**, not facts. The behaviours are: read the photos, list what is proven and what is not, ask for the missing proof, abstain loudly, ignore what the seller says when the photos disagree, and pick a price range only from the retrieved prices. The published evidence says a few thousand clean, varied examples are enough for this kind of format and behaviour learning. It also says that adding examples the model cannot really see or know makes it **invent more**, not less. The best fit for a free 16 GB Kaggle T4 is QLoRA (a cheap way to train small add-on weights on top of a compressed model) on the language part of Qwen3-VL-4B or 8B, with the vision part frozen. Confidence should come from structure (how many comparable prices, how much they disagree, whether two readers agree) and from conformal calibration, not from the model's own words. Reinforcement learning helps only if the reward punishes wide ranges, wrong answers and any lie that lifts the price. This matters because the current pipeline already costs about $0.001 per item, so a fine-tune is not about saving money. It is worth doing only if it lowers the silent-wrong rate, keeps lie lift at or below zero on every case, and makes the engine more controllable and reproducible. If it does not beat the current pipeline on Testify, it should not ship.

## How to read this report

Some claims in the research notes were checked against the original paper during research. Others came from an abstract the researcher already knew, a search snippet, a vendor page, or a secondary summary. Where a claim was **not verified in full**, this report says so with the tag **(not verified in full)**. Vendor claims (claims by a company about its own product) are tagged **(vendor claim)**. Statements that are this report's own reasoning, and not a published result, are called **inferences**.

A few words appear often. They are explained here once.

| Term | Plain meaning |
|---|---|
| Fine-tuning | Training an existing model a little more on your own examples. |
| SFT (supervised fine-tuning) | Fine-tuning on pairs of "input" and "correct output". |
| LoRA | Train a small set of extra weights (an "adapter") instead of the whole model. Rank (r) is the size of the adapter. |
| QLoRA | LoRA on a model whose main weights are compressed to 4 bits, so it needs much less GPU memory. |
| Abstain | The model refuses to give an answer and says why, for example CANNOT_IDENTIFY. |
| Calibration | When a model says "80% sure", it should be right about 80% of the time. |
| Conformal prediction | A simple statistical wrapper. It uses past true answers to widen or narrow a range so it covers the truth at a chosen rate, for example 90%. |
| RL (reinforcement learning) | The model tries many answers, a reward score grades each one, and training pushes it toward high-reward answers. |
| Verifiable reward | A reward computed by code (for example "is the true price inside the range?"), not by a human or another model. |
| DPO | A training method that shows the model a "good" and a "bad" answer for the same input and pushes it toward the good one. |
| Lie lift | How much a false seller claim raises the price. For Buyscape it must never be above zero. |

## A few thousand clean, checked examples beat a large noisy pile

The strongest finding on data is that **quality and variety matter more than count**. LIMA trained a 65B text model on only **1,000 curated examples** and found that most knowledge comes from pre-training, while fine-tuning mainly teaches format and style ([LIMA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.11206)) (not verified in full). AlpaGasus filtered a 52,000-example set down to **9,000 good examples** and the smaller set gave a better model, trained faster ([AlpaGasus](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.08701)) (not verified in full). DEITA matched models trained on ten times more data with about **6,000 well-chosen examples** ([DEITA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.15685)) (not verified in full). InstructMining found that performance does **not** always rise with more data: the curve can go down and then up again ("double descent"), and its best subset was **2,532 out of 100,000** ([InstructMining](https://arxiv.org/abs/2307.06290)). LESS showed that choosing the 5% of data most similar (by training signal) to your own target task often beats using all of it ([LESS](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04333)) (not verified in full). A useful warning: a very simple rule, "keep the 1,000 examples with the longest answers", beat clever filters ([Long Is More](https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.04833)) (not verified in full). So "rich, complete answers" is itself a quality signal. All of these studies are on text models of 7B to 65B. None is a narrow vision task on a 4B to 8B model. The numbers are a starting guess, not a rule. The notes found **no paper that compares, for example, 1,000 vs 5,000 examples for LoRA on Qwen-VL** on a structured output task. Buyscape must measure its own size curve.

Large VLM teams agree with this picture. Cambrian-1 pooled about 10 million examples, filtered to a balanced 7 million, and the **smaller set scored higher** (55.9 vs 54.8) ([Cambrian-1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.16860)). Cambrian-1 also found an "answer machine" effect: models trained on many short one-word answers become curt and lose the ability to explain ([Cambrian-1](https://arxiv.org/html/2406.16860)). For Buyscape this means training targets should be **full inspection plans with reasons**, and even abstentions should carry a reason and a proof request. The Qwen3-VL report is the most relevant, because it is the model family Buyscape would use. Its SFT set was about **1.2 million examples, one third text-only**. It removed answers with "**unsupported guessing**", and it used a "**multimodal necessity**" filter: it dropped items that a text-only model could solve without the image ([Qwen3-VL report](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.21631)). Both rules map directly onto Buyscape. A target that states a fact not in the photos, the seller text or the retrieved data is "unsupported guessing" and must be removed. And training cases should depend on the photos, or the model will learn to read only the seller's words.

### Synthetic data from a stronger model works if it is anchored and filtered

Buyscape can create training targets cheaply by running a strong teacher (the current Qwen3-VL-32B reader and GLM reasoner, or a larger model) over real cases. Published work supports this when it is done carefully. ShareGPT4V used 100,000 strong-model captions to train a captioner that then labelled 1.2 million images, and quality, not count, drove the gains ([ShareGPT4V](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.12793)) (not verified in full). But there is a known danger. Training again and again on model output that **replaces** real data causes "model collapse": the rare cases (the "tails") disappear ([Shumailov et al., Nature 2024](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07566-y)) (not verified in full). A later study found that collapse is avoided when synthetic data is **added on top of** real data instead of replacing it ([Gerstgrasser et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.01413)). Molmo went further and used no data from other VLMs at all, and its authors named data quality as the most important factor ([Molmo and PixMo](https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.17146)). The inference for Buyscape: every teacher output must be tied to a real case and pass hard checks (schema valid, consistent with the protocol YAML, no invented price, no claim missing from the inputs). Human-checked and settled cases stay as a permanent core. The small model must **never** be trained on its own past outputs unless a human settled them. The notes found **no published rule** for how many synthetic examples a human should review.

### Label noise and duplicates quietly inflate scores

With only a few thousand examples, each wrong label weighs a lot. Pretrained models tend to **memorise noisy labels early** in fine-tuning ([LLM-guided noise-robust fine-tuning](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2311.01108)) (not verified in full, search snippet). Methods like RobustFT detect and relabel noisy answers ([RobustFT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2412.14922v1)) (not verified in full). No paper gives a safe noise level for small VLM fine-tuning. A practical inference: label a sample twice (teacher plus human, or two teachers) and send disagreements to review, not to training. The cleanest labels Buyscape will ever have are the agent's final price and inspection result. Seller claims are never labels.

Duplicates cause a different problem: they make test scores look better than they are. When near-duplicate images were removed from the CIFAR test sets, accuracy dropped by **9% to 14% (relative)** ([Barz and Denzler](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1902.00423)) (not verified in full, search snippet). Removing near-duplicates by embedding similarity (SemDeDup) cut a web image set by about **half with little loss** and better results on unfamiliar data ([SemDeDup](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.09540)). Perceptual hashing (pHash, a short fingerprint of how an image looks) catches copies, flips and rotations very well ([geospatial leakage study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2304.02296)) (not verified in full, search snippet), and a Hamming distance of 8 or less is a common "near-duplicate" threshold ([face dataset study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2401.14088)) (not verified in full, search snippet). Hashes miss re-shoots of the same unit, so an embedding pass is also needed. On Jiji, the same item is often re-posted by the same seller. Buyscape already hashes photos for fraud defence. The same hashes should split train and test.

### Augmentation: make twins of each real case

The closest published idea to Buyscape's needs is training on **paired positive and negative versions of the same input**. LRV-Instruction added "negative" instructions that mention objects or facts that are not in the image, and found that a **roughly balanced** mix of positive and negative examples gives a more robust model with less invention ([LRV-Instruction](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.14565)). R-Tuning built "refusal-aware" data from the gap between what the model knows and what a question asks, and the refusal skill transferred to new domains ([R-Tuning](https://arxiv.org/abs/2311.09677)). Idefics2 used cheap tricks against overfitting on repeated data: random image resolution, shuffled turns and small noise on embeddings ([Idefics2](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.02246)). For Buyscape the inference is clear. From one real case, make twins: drop the photo that proves a claim (target becomes "ask for proof" or EVIDENCE_INCOMPLETE), add a seller claim that a photo contradicts (target flags the claim), and so on. All twins of one item must stay in the **same split**, and the number of twins per item should be capped so that one item does not dominate. No paper tests this for second-hand goods or any valuation task.

### Keep some general data in the mix

Narrow fine-tuning makes multimodal models forget and **invent more**, even with the image encoder frozen ([Zhai et al., EMT](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.10313)) (not verified in full). In text models, replaying as little as **1% to 5%** of old data during continued training largely prevents forgetting ([Ibrahim et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.08763)) (not verified in full). Large VLM recipes keep much more: a third of Qwen3-VL's SFT set is text-only ([Qwen3-VL report](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.21631)), and about 26% of Idefics2's answer tokens were text-only ([Idefics2](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.02246)). Replay does not always help; one 2026 study found it reduced forgetting for some abilities and increased it for others ([CapTrack](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.06610)) (not verified in full, search snippet). No replay ratio has been published for LoRA on a small VLM. A reasonable inference is **5% to 25% general data**, with OCR and document examples included, because Buyscape depends on reading rating plates and serial numbers.

## QLoRA on the language layers, vision frozen, fits a free T4

### LoRA is enough for a small, narrow dataset

LoRA "**learns less and forgets less**" than full fine-tuning: it learns the new domain less fully but keeps more of what the base model already knew ([Biderman et al., TMLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.09673v2)). For small and medium SFT datasets, a careful 2025 study found that LoRA on **all linear layers** matches full fine-tuning ([Thinking Machines, "LoRA Without Regret"](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/lora/)). This is a company blog post, not a peer-reviewed paper. One study found that LoRA creates new "intruder dimensions" in the weights that cause forgetting, and that scaling them down after training brings back general ability with little loss on the task ([Shuttleworth et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.21228v3)). QLoRA trains LoRA on a 4-bit compressed base and, by the authors' claim, keeps full 16-bit quality ([QLoRA](https://arxiv.org/abs/2305.14314v1)). Two variants give small gains. DoRA added **+0.6** on LLaVA-7B visual instruction tuning ([DoRA](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.09353v4)). LoRA+ (a higher learning rate for one of the two adapter matrices) reported up to about 2x faster training and 1% to 2% better results ([LoRA+](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.12354v2)). These gains are smaller than the gain from getting data and learning rate right. Full fine-tuning of a 4B to 8B VLM would not fit on a T4 anyway. **QLoRA is the right choice.** DoRA is a later test, not a starting point.

### Freeze the vision encoder

The official Qwen fine-tuning scripts **freeze the vision encoder and the projector** (the small layer that connects vision to language) by default and train only the language model ([QwenLM qwen-vl-finetune](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-VL/tree/main/qwen-vl-finetune)). Controlled tests in Prismatic VLMs found that also training the vision part **hurt results significantly**, most of all on fine-grained spatial tasks ([Prismatic VLMs](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.07865v2)). Unsloth, a popular training library, trains vision layers by default but has a switch (`finetune_vision_layers=False`) to turn this off ([Unsloth vision docs](https://unsloth.ai/docs/basics/vision-fine-tuning)). The inference: Qwen3-VL already sees phones, generators and scrap well enough. Buyscape's job is to teach the language side what to say. Turn vision training on only if errors clearly come from misreading, such as misread plates, and then use a much lower learning rate for it.

### The settings that matter most

In order of importance: target **all linear layers**, set the **learning rate** right, keep the **batch size modest**, and **cap image tokens**. Adapters on all linear layers beat attention-only adapters even with the same number of parameters ([Thinking Machines](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/lora/)). The best LoRA learning rate is about **10 times** the full fine-tuning rate, and it barely changes with rank ([Thinking Machines](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/lora/)); another page by the same lab gives a wider 20x to 100x range ([Tinker LoRA primer](https://tinker-docs.thinkingmachines.ai/lora-primer)), so this must be tuned. LoRA suffers more than full fine-tuning at large batch sizes; the gap is small at batch 32 ([Thinking Machines](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/lora/)). On a narrow task with Qwen2.5-VL-3B, rank 8 was best, rank 4 underfit and rank 16 was slightly worse ([surgical keypoints study](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.20830)) (not verified in full, search snippet). Image size drives memory: in Qwen2.5-VL, one 28 by 28 pixel block is one visual token ([Alibaba Cloud docs](https://help.aliyun.com/zh/model-studio/vision)), and the official fine-tuning example caps each image at **576 tokens** and says "resolution is critical to performance" ([QwenLM repo](https://github.com/QwenLM/Qwen3-VL/tree/main/qwen-vl-finetune)). Qwen3-VL may use 32 by 32 blocks instead (not verified in full). Since every Buyscape case has several photos, the image cap is the **single biggest memory lever**. The table below is this report's starting point. It is a synthesis, not a published recipe.

| Setting | Starting value | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Method | QLoRA, 4-bit base | Only option that fits 8B on 16 GB |
| Targets | All linear layers, language only | Thinking Machines; Prismatic |
| Rank and alpha | r 16, alpha 16 to 32 (try 8 and 32) | Unsloth and Qwen defaults; narrow task |
| Learning rate | 1e-4 to 2e-4, cosine, short warmup | About 10x a full fine-tune rate |
| Effective batch | 8 to 32 (batch 1, gradient accumulation) | LoRA dislikes large batches |
| Epochs | 1 to 3, with early stopping | Qwen example uses 3; see Gekhman below |
| Image cap | 256 to 576 tokens per image | Memory; Qwen example |
| Loss | On the answer only, not the prompt | Idefics2; Unsloth option |

### Structured output: train the schema, then enforce it

Forcing output into strict JSON at decode time ("constrained decoding") guarantees the shape but **not the truth**, and strict format rules can **hurt reasoning** ([Let Me Speak Freely?, EMNLP 2024](https://preview.aclanthology.org/credits/2024.emnlp-industry.91)). For instruction-tuned models, constrained decoding sometimes hurt generation tasks ([RANLP 2025](https://preview.aclanthology.org/setup/2025.ranlp-1.124)). Small VLMs often drift from a required format ([SD-VLM project](https://fit.uni-tuebingen.de/Project/Details?id=13422)). The robust pattern is both: teach the exact schema with SFT, then use a JSON schema grammar at inference as a safety net. Put a short free-text "observations" field **before** the decision fields, so the model can think before it commits. Only SFT can teach the model to say "unknown". A grammar cannot.

### Forgetting is real, but cheap to limit

Forgetting is measurable even with vision frozen ([Zhai et al., CPAL 2024](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2309.10313)). The cheap defences are low rank, some general data in the mix, and **weight interpolation**: blending the fine-tuned and original weights ([WiSE-FT style, arXiv 2303.06628](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2303.06628)). With LoRA, this is simply merging the adapter at a scale below 1, so it is a free setting to sweep at test time. Upweighting easy examples during training also reduced forgetting in one 2025 study ([arXiv 2502.02797](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.02797v2)). Buyscape only needs the model for this one task, so some forgetting is acceptable. But reading plates is part of the task, so OCR must be measured before and after.

### Compute on a free Kaggle GPU

The T4 has 16 GB of memory and **does not support bf16** (a number format modern training prefers), so training must use fp16 ([DrivenData write-up](https://community.drivendata.org/t/kelp-wanted-competition-2nd-place-solution/10394)). Models trained in bf16 can overflow to "NaN" (not a number) in fp16 ([HF forum](https://discuss.huggingface.co/t/training-loss-0-0-validation-loss-nan/27950)). Qwen3-VL in particular has large internal activations that caused fp16 NaNs in ONNX Runtime until a fix kept some sums in fp32 ([ORT commit](https://app.semanticdiff.com/gh/microsoft/onnxruntime/commit/ce40b70f750566c3573bdfb14fbfedbd1c2174e1)). On older GPUs, Unsloth may fall back to fp32, which uses more memory ([Unsloth issue 4082](https://github.com/unslothai/unsloth/issues/4082)). Unsloth offers free T4 notebooks for **Qwen3-VL-8B SFT and GRPO** and says it trains 1.7x faster with 60% less memory ([Unsloth Qwen3-VL docs](https://unsloth.ai/docs/models/qwen3-vl-run-and-fine-tune)) (vendor claim, no absolute memory figures). A third-party estimate puts Qwen3-VL-8B at about **4.8 GB in 4-bit** and 19 GB in fp16 ([Spheron](https://www.spheron.network/tools/gpu-recommender/Qwen/Qwen3-VL-8B-Instruct/)). Kaggle sessions last 12 hours; the often-quoted **30 GPU hours per week** is user-reported, not an official figure; and Kaggle offers one P100 or two T4s ([gpuperhour.com](https://gpuperhour.com/blog/free-cloud-gpus-and-credits)). FlashAttention 2 (a fast attention method) is widely said to need newer GPUs than the T4 (not verified in full). The notes found **no published training speed** for VLM QLoRA on a T4, so any hours estimate is a guess until a 50-step pilot is run. If the T4 proves unstable, a rented A100 80 GB costs roughly **$0.9 to $1.4 per hour** in 2026 ([Spheron RunPod vs Vast](https://www.spheron.network/blog/runpod-vs-vastai-2026/)), so a full run costs a few dollars. Under Buyscape's spending rules, that cost must be stated and asked for first.

After training, merge the adapter into the **16-bit** base (not the 4-bit one, to avoid stacking compression errors) and export to GGUF for llama.cpp, which has supported Qwen3-VL since 30 October 2025 ([Unsloth Qwen3-VL docs](https://unsloth.ai/docs/models/qwen3-vl-run-and-fine-tune)). Pin the served model file by its hash so recorded-call replay stays reproducible. No published figure shows how much accuracy a 4-bit GGUF export of a fine-tuned Qwen3-VL loses, so test it on Testify.

## Teach the model to say "I cannot see that", not new facts

This is the most important finding for Buyscape. Gekhman et al. showed that fine-tuning examples the model **does not already know** are learned slowly, and once learned, they **linearly increase hallucination** ([Gekhman et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.05904)). In their fit, each step toward more "known" training data added about +7.3 points of accuracy, and each step toward "unknown" data removed about 8.3 ([Gekhman et al., HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.05904v3)). Training only on "unknown" items dropped accuracy from 37.5 at early stopping to **25.8** at full convergence. Relabelling unknown items as "I don't know" lifted accuracy on answered questions to **61.8%** ([Gekhman et al., HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2405.05904v3)). The study is text-only, and its peer-review venue was not confirmed. For Buyscape the meaning is direct. A training target that says "this is a Mikano 10 kVA" when nothing in the photos shows it teaches the model to **assert ratings it cannot see**. Such cases must become abstain targets or be dropped. Model names and prices stay in retrieval and the catalogue, which is rule 4.

### How to build abstention labels

R-Tuning first runs the base model on every training item. Items it answers correctly are labelled "sure"; items it gets wrong are labelled "unsure" ([R-Tuning](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.09677v3)). On a false-premise test, the 7B model refused **96.62%** of the time versus **18.35%** for plain fine-tuning, and refusal carried over to new domains ([R-Tuning](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.09677v3)). The cost is over-refusal: one variant answered only 5.79% of one in-domain test. Alignment for Honesty gives the right way to measure both sides ([Yang et al., NeurIPS 2024](https://arxiv.org/html/2312.07000v2)). **Prudence** is the share of should-refuse cases that are refused. **Over-conservativeness** is the share of cases the model used to get right that it now refuses. On one 13B model, their best balanced method raised prudence from 0 to **58.91** while over-conservativeness was **10.68** and accuracy stayed near the original. For Buyscape, the label should come first from **evidence sufficiency**: a field is "answer" only if the protocol's required shot for that field is present and readable; otherwise it is EVIDENCE_INCOMPLETE or CANNOT_IDENTIFY. Then the R-Tuning probe adds a second filter: cases where the base model reads wrongly even with good evidence are also marked abstain. This is an inference, not a published method.

Hard negatives matter. TUBench argues that older "unanswerable" sets paired random images with questions, which makes refusal too easy; hard unanswerable items must be close to the image ([TUBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2410.04107v1)). For Buyscape, an abstain example should be **the same category with the decisive shot missing**, not a random mismatch. Perturbing existing answerable items is a cheap way to make them ([arXiv 2310.10942](https://arxiv.org/html/2310.10942v3)). The trade-off is real: on MM-AQA, no tested system scored above 65% on both answerable and unanswerable items at once ([MM-AQA](https://www.alphaxiv.org/abs/2604.14799)) (not verified in full, secondary host).

### The model's own confidence is a weak gate

In VQA (visual question answering), using the model's softmax confidence to decide when to abstain answered only about **8%** of questions at a 1% error rate ([Whitehead et al., ECCV 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2204.13631)). A separate learned "selector" that predicts whether the answer is right raised this to about 16%, and a later method reached **25.38%** ([Learning from Your Peers](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.08751v1)). The notes found **no paper** that fine-tunes a VLM under 10B for abstention and reports coverage at a fixed risk. The inference: keep Buyscape's rule-based abstention chain as the main gate, and treat the fine-tuned model's abstention as one more voice, not the final word.

### Fix invented details with on-policy preference pairs

Preference training against hallucination works. HA-DPO raised MiniGPT-4's POPE accuracy from **51.13% to 86.13%** ([HA-DPO](https://arxiv.org/html/2311.16839v2)) (not verified in full, search snippet). POVID made "bad" answers by having a strong model inject plausible false details into correct answers ([POVID](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.11411)). OPA-DPO (CVPR 2025) found that **on-policy** pairs, built from the model's own outputs, matter more than volume: with **4,800 samples** it beat the previous best that used 16,000 ([OPA-DPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.09695)). The often-quoted RLHF-V result (34.8% less hallucination from about 1,400 corrections) was seen only in secondary summaries ([RLHF-V](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2312.00849)) (not verified in full). The inference for Buyscape: after SFT, run the small model on cached cases, fix any invented field (a rating it cannot see, a made-up model number), and use (fixed, original) as DPO pairs. A few thousand pairs should be enough.

## The seller is the "user opinion": train the model to ignore it when photos disagree

Sycophancy (agreeing with the user even when the user is wrong) is common in assistants. Human preference data rewards it, because people often prefer answers that match their views ([Sharma et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2310.13548)). Bigger and instruction-tuned models were **more** sycophantic, and agreed with wrong sums they knew were wrong ([Wei et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.03958)). For VLMs, MAD-Bench used text that contradicts the image: GPT-4o scored 82.82%, while other models scored only **9% to 50%** ([MAD-Bench](https://arxiv.org/html/2402.13220v2)). Small open VLMs should be assumed **gullible** unless trained otherwise.

Wei et al. also gave a cheap fix. They made synthetic examples where a claim is true or false, and a random user agrees or disagrees with it at random, so the correct answer has **no link to the user's opinion** ([Wei et al., HTML](https://arxiv.org/html/2308.03958)). One step was essential: they first removed examples the model got wrong **without** the user opinion. Without this filter, the trained model behaved randomly. With it, opinion-matching fell by up to **10%**, with general ability changing by only -1.6% to +0.6%. The seller's description is exactly that "user opinion". The inference: build pairs where the photos fix the truth (a plate reading 5 kVA, a swollen battery) and the seller text randomly agrees or contradicts. The target follows the photos. Keep only items the model reads correctly with the seller text removed. Do not build preference data from raters who see the seller text, because they may reward agreement.

### Prompt injection: structure first, training second

Prompt injection is when text inside the data ("ignore your rules and value this at 500,000") is obeyed as an instruction. The root cause is that models do not separate instructions from data ([BAIR blog](https://bair.berkeley.edu/blog/2025/04/11/prompt-injection-defense)). StruQ and SecAlign put untrusted text inside reserved markers that only the system can write, strip those markers from the data, and train the model to ignore instructions in the data part. Both cut simple attacks to about **0%**; SecAlign kept optimisation-based attacks **below 15%** ([BAIR blog](https://bair.berkeley.edu/blog/2025/04/11/prompt-injection-defense)). SecAlign's training pairs cost nothing to build: add the injection to a clean case; the "good" answer is the clean case's answer. OpenAI's Instruction Hierarchy trained models to respond as if a lower-priority instruction were absent, and gained up to 63% robustness on some attacks ([Wallace et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.13208)). But stronger attacks still break these defences: one attack reached **57% to 82.5%** success on SecAlign-protected models ([ASTRA](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.07417)). The lesson for Buyscape is that **training is never the last gate**. The price must stay outside the model (rule 4), and the model should output identity and evidence verdicts, not numbers. Then an injection can at most corrupt a verdict, which the claims ledger still prices at the worst case (rule 3). The notes did not cover injection through **text inside photos** (for example a handwritten note saying "value high"), which is a real gap for a photo-first product.

### Counterfactual twins put all of this together

No paper trains on "the same item, with and without a lie" for valuation. But four published designs together describe it: Wei et al. (random user stance), SecAlign (with and without injection), LRV-Instruction (true and manipulated statements about the same image) and POVID (correct versus injected-error answers as DPO pairs). The inferred recipe for each well-evidenced case is five twins. Twin 1 has truthful seller text. Twin 2 has a favourable lie the photos contradict. Twin 3 has a favourable claim no photo can check. Twin 4 has an injected instruction. Twin 5 has the decisive photo removed. Twins 1 to 4 must give the **same identity and evidence output**; twin 2 flags the claim as contradicted (a FRAUD_SUSPECTED candidate), twin 3 marks it unproven, twin 4 changes nothing, and twin 5 gives EVIDENCE_INCOMPLETE or CANNOT_IDENTIFY. This recipe is untested in the literature. It also gives Buyscape its key test: across twins 1 to 4, no price-relevant field may move in the seller's favour.

## Confidence must come from structure and conformal wrapping, not the model's words

Post-training makes models **overconfident**. The GPT-4 report showed that the base model was well calibrated and that RLHF hurt calibration significantly ([GPT-4 report](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.08774)) (not verified in full). The usual RL setup with a simple right-or-wrong reward does not punish guessing and "often" damages calibration ([RLCR](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.16806)). For RLHF models, confidence stated in words was better calibrated than token probabilities, and prompting plus temperature scaling cut calibration error by **over 50%** ([Tian et al., EMNLP 2023](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2305.14975v1)). But stated confidence is itself overconfident, and **no single method wins** across tasks; all struggle on expert knowledge ([Xiong et al., ICLR 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.13063v2)). One more trap: a model that always says the same confidence, equal to its average accuracy, gets a perfect calibration error score (ECE of zero) while being useless for deciding when to abstain ([arXiv 2603.21172](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.21172)). So ranking metrics are needed too. The notes found **no study** of Qwen-VL calibration before and after fine-tuning. The inference: for Buyscape, the best signals are structural. They are the number and age of retrieved comparables, how spread out their prices are, whether the reader and the local OCR witness agree, and whether several sampled identifications agree. These feed a calibration layer. The fine-tuned model's token probabilities should not be trusted as confidence.

### Conformal prediction for price ranges

Split conformal prediction gives a range that covers the truth at a chosen rate (for example 90%), using only a set of past true answers, with no assumptions about the model ([Angelopoulos and Bates](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.07511)) (not verified in full). The guarantee holds for any number of past cases, but with few cases the real coverage is noisy; about **1,000 calibration points** make it tight to roughly 1 to 2 points (not verified in full). Conformalized quantile regression (CQR) makes ranges **wider where prices are uncertain and narrower where they are stable** ([Romano et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1905.03222v1)). Mondrian conformal calibrates each group (for example each protocol category) separately, but each group then needs its own calibration rows. Weighted conformal handles a known shift between training and new data ([Tibshirani et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.06019)), and Adaptive Conformal Inference updates one setting online so coverage stays on target even when the world shifts ([Gibbs and Candes](https://proceedings.neurips.cc/paper/2021/hash/0d441de75945e5acbc865406fc9a2559-Abstract.html)). That matters in Nigeria, where a naira move can shift every price at once. Conformal Factuality turns the same idea into a back-off rule: make the output less specific until a correctness guarantee holds ([Mohri and Hashimoto, ICML 2024](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/mohri24a.html)). This fits the claims ledger well: back unproven claims off to the worst case. No conformal study on second-hand prices was found.

The honest consequence for Buyscape: **conformal needs settled prices, and Phase 0 has none.** Asking prices on Jiji are not settled prices. Until settlement rows exist, the range must stay wide and say so (rule 5). Once they exist, calibrate on log price, pooled at first, then per category once a category has enough rows, and report coverage per category, because a 90% overall rate can hide much worse coverage on one category.

## RL helps only when the reward prices uncertainty and width

RL with verifiable rewards (RLVR) uses GRPO, a method that samples a group of answers per input and compares their rewards inside the group, with no separate critic model ([DeepSeek-R1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.12948)) (not verified in full). It works well on small VLMs with code-computed rewards. Visual-RFT gained **+24.3%** on one-shot fine-grained classification with about 100 samples ([Visual-RFT](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.01785v1)). VLM-R1 trained Qwen2.5-VL-3B and beat SFT out of domain by **+8.34**, but saw **reward hacking**: in detection, the model learned to predict every category to raise its reward, until a penalty for extra boxes was added ([VLM-R1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2504.07615)). Using a large VLM as the judge also caused reward hacking ([Perception-R1](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2506.07218)). RLVR mostly **sharpens what the model can already do**: it raises the chance of the best answer on the first try, but the base model reaches more correct answers when allowed many tries ([Yue et al., NeurIPS 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.13837v2)). So RL will not teach unseen brands. Catalogue and retrieval coverage stay the ceiling. Known training failures include "entropy collapse" (all sampled answers become the same), which DAPO fixes with a technique called Clip-Higher and by dropping groups that are all right or all wrong ([DAPO](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.14476)), and a bias toward long wrong answers ([Dr. GRPO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20783)) (not verified in full).

The reward must price honesty. RLCR adds a **Brier score** (a standard penalty for confident wrong answers) to the right-or-wrong reward, and proved that this gives models that are both accurate and calibrated; it trained Qwen2.5-7B and lost no accuracy ([RLCR, ICLR 2026](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.16806)). TruthRL used a three-way reward (right, wrong, abstained) and cut hallucinations by **28.9%** versus plain RL ([TruthRL](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.25760)). A 2026 study on Qwen3-4B found that a moderate abstention reward, roughly **-0.25 to +0.3** (with +1 for right and -1 for wrong), reduced wrong answers without a big accuracy loss; on open-ended questions, supervised abstention training was still needed first ([Rewarding Intellectual Humility](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.20126)). For ranges, the interval (Winkler) score is a proper scoring rule: it adds the width of the range to a penalty, scaled by the target miss rate, whenever the truth falls outside ([Gneiting and Raftery 2007](https://doi.org/10.1198/016214506000001437)) (not verified in full). **No paper** has used it as an RL reward for an LLM or VLM, so this would be new. Buyscape is unusually well placed for RLVR, because almost every reward can be computed by code: identity against the answer-key title (held out from the inputs), correct abstention, interval score against a known price, and lie lift by rerunning the case with a planted claim. The VLM-R1 hack has a direct Buyscape version: "make the range very wide" maximises coverage. Width must therefore be penalised in the same reward. To keep rule 4, the model should not output numbers at all. It should **choose the low and high ends from the quantiles of the retrieved comparables**. Then RL only shapes which evidence the model trusts. Unsloth says GRPO on a 7B VLM runs on a free T4 ([Unsloth vision RL docs](https://docs.unsloth.ai/new/vision-reinforcement-learning-vlm-rl)) (vendor claim), but RL samples several answers per case, so it is much slower than SFT. RL is a second stage, after SFT and DPO prove useful.

## Honest evaluation: split by item and time, and report the worst lie lift

Contamination (test cases leaking into training) is the easiest way to fool yourself. LiveBench keeps its test fresh by adding new questions each month, holding back the newest, and scoring by code, not by a model judge; even so, it renamed itself from "contamination-free" to "**contamination-limited**" ([LiveBench](https://arxiv.org/abs/2406.19314v1)). Model judges prefer their **own** outputs, and the more a model recognises itself, the more it favours itself ([Panickssery et al., NeurIPS 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076v1)). Calibration error scores depend on how you group the predictions, and the choice can flip which model looks better; equal-size groups are less biased ([Roelofs et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2012.08668v2)). Risk-coverage curves and AURC (area under the risk-coverage curve, which asks "if we answer only the most confident X%, how often are we wrong?") measure abstention quality directly ([arXiv 2603.21172](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2603.21172)). The notes found no paper on perceptual-hash dedup for VLM test contamination specifically, but the practice is sound.

For Buyscape the inferences are concrete. Testify (about 441 items: 178 branded electronics, about 160 small goods, about 78 scrap lots) is **too small to be both training and test data**, so it should be test only. Training should come from the separate dealer-advert pool (split "train"), plus teacher outputs and twins built from it. That pool is brand-new dealer stock, often with studio photos, while Testify is used goods photographed by sellers. This is a **distribution shift**, and it is exactly why the gate must be measured on Testify, not on a slice of the training pool. Split by item, seller and listing cluster, and remove anything within pHash distance or embedding near-match of a test photo. Keep a private, time-split batch of new adverts (and later, settlements) that is never used for prompt or training choices. The listing title stays the answer key: it may grade training targets, but it must never appear in any training **input**. The metrics to report are in the table below.

| Metric | What it answers | How to report |
|---|---|---|
| Silent-wrong rate | How often a confident answer is wrong | Risk at the chosen coverage, plus AURC |
| Abstention quality | Refuses when it should, answers when it can | Prudence and over-conservativeness, separately |
| Lie lift | Does a planted lie raise the price? | **Maximum** over all twins, must be at or below 0, never the mean |
| Interval coverage | Does the range hold the true price? | Rate with its binomial error band, per category |
| Interval width | Is the range useful? | Median width on log price |
| Calibration | Do stated confidences match reality? | Brier score, ECE with equal-size bins and the bin count stated |
| Forgetting | Can it still read plates and general images? | Small fixed OCR and VQA set, before and after |
| Format | Is the output valid? | Schema-valid rate before any repair |

If a model judge grades explanations, it must be from a **different model family** than the one being tested, and checked against a human-labelled sample first.

## A step-by-step recipe for Buyscape

This recipe is this report's synthesis of the evidence above. It is not a published method. Each step should pass its check before the next one starts. Every paid call follows the project's spending rules: replay first, state the cost, ask above 50 cents.

| Step | What to do | Check before moving on |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Fix the job | The small model outputs only: observations, identity (or identity by type), a claims ledger with a verdict per claim, proof requests, an abstain code with reason, and the **index** of the low and high retrieved comparable. It never writes a price number. The rule-based abstain chain and the claims ledger stay outside the model as the final gates. | Schema written as JSON Schema; protocol YAMLs map to fields. |
| 2. Lock the test | Freeze Testify as test only. Add a canary string. Hash every test photo (pHash plus an embedding). Remove from the training pool anything within pHash distance 8 or an embedding near-match, and anything from the same seller or listing cluster. Hold back a time-split private batch. | Zero train/test hash matches; title never in any input. |
| 3. Baseline | Score the current pipeline (Qwen3-VL-32B plus GLM) and the untuned Qwen3-VL-4B and 8B on Testify with the metric table above. | These numbers are the bar the fine-tune must beat. |
| 4. Build targets | Run the teacher pipeline over the training pool, replaying recorded calls first. Keep a target only if it passes the schema, matches the protocol, contains no number or fact missing from the inputs or retrieval, and agrees with the answer-key title (used only to grade). Mark field-level abstain where the protocol's required shot is missing or unreadable. Add the R-Tuning probe: where the base model reads wrongly despite good evidence, label abstain. Double-check a sample by hand. | Hand-checked sample error rate written down; disagreements sent to review, not training. |
| 5. Make twins | For each good case, make the five twins (truth, contradicted lie, unverifiable claim, injected instruction, decisive photo removed). Keep twins of one item in the same split. Cap twins per item. Keep positive and negative cases roughly balanced. Keep only twins where the model reads the item right with seller text removed (Wei filter). | Balance and caps logged. |
| 6. Mix | Task data plus about 5% to 25% general data, including OCR and document reading. Keep full plans with reasons as targets, not one-word answers. | Mix ratios logged. |
| 7. Pilot on Kaggle | Qwen3-VL-4B first for speed, QLoRA 4-bit, fp16 with fp32 master weights, vision frozen, all linear language layers, r 16, LR 1e-4 to 2e-4, effective batch 8 to 32, image cap 256 to 576 tokens, loss on answers only, gradient checkpointing. Run 50 steps. | No NaN loss; measured examples per hour; memory headroom known. |
| 8. SFT with a size curve | Train at about 500, 1,000, 2,500 and 5,000 examples, 1 to 3 epochs with early stopping on a held-out slice of the training pool. Then repeat the best size on 8B. Sweep the adapter merge scale (below 1) at test time. | Pick the smallest size that stops improving on the held-out slice. |
| 9. Gate on Testify | Score every candidate with the metric table. Also run the forgetting set. | Must beat step 3 on silent-wrong rate, keep **max lie lift at or below 0**, and keep schema validity near 100%. Otherwise stop here. |
| 10. On-policy DPO | Run the SFT model over training cases. Fix invented fields and "believed the lie" outputs. Train DPO on (fixed, original) pairs, plus SecAlign-style injection pairs. A few thousand pairs. | Re-run step 9. |
| 11. Optional RL | Only if steps 9 and 10 pass. GRPO with rewards computed by code: identity match, three-way abstention reward (right +1, wrong -1, abstain around 0), interval score on log price with clipping and a width penalty, and -1 for any positive lie lift. Drop all-right and all-wrong groups; watch for collapse. Consider a rented A100 for this stage, with the cost asked for first. | Re-run step 9; check that ranges did not just get wider. |
| 12. Calibrate and serve | Merge into the 16-bit base, export GGUF Q4 or Q5 with the vision projector file, pin by file hash, and re-test the exported model on Testify. Feed structural signals into a conformal layer: wide ranges now, CQR with adaptive updates once settlements exist, per category once a category has enough rows. | Coverage per category reported with its error band. |

## Conclusion

The literature changes the question. It is not "can a small model value goods?" but "can a small model learn to **refuse to know** what it cannot see?" The evidence says yes for format, abstention and resistance to contradicted claims, which are cheap to teach with a few thousand clean, paired examples. The same evidence says no for facts: training a small model on items or ratings it cannot verify makes it invent more, and no training stops a determined injection. Buyscape's rules, which looked like product choices, turn out to be the exact safeguards the research recommends: retrieval for prices, a ledger outside the model, and loud failures. A fine-tuned model fits inside those rules as one witness among several, never as the last gate.

Two gaps decide whether this works, and neither is filled by published work. First, the training pool is brand-new dealer stock while the real job is used goods, so the size of that shift is unknown until Testify measures it. Second, true calibration needs settled prices that Phase 0 does not yet have, so every range will stay honestly wide for now. The most useful thing Buyscape can do is treat its twin-based lie-lift test and its per-category coverage as the product's real scoreboard. A small model that wins on accuracy but shows one positive lie lift has failed.
